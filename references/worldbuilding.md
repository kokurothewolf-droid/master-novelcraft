# Worldbuilding and place as lived experience

Sources: `worldbuilding(1).md` (also duplicated under the misleading filename `fiction-editor(1).md`), Moore place/time guidance P05–P09, prose-setting techniques.

## World facts and story effects

For every important locale, capture physical environment, history, power, customs, language, economy, transport, climate, sensory texture, and who regards that place as home, threat, aspiration, or prison. Real places and times require reliable research when accuracy matters; when unavailable, flag unverified specifics. Made-up places need **internal consistency**, not imitation of reality.

Build lived texture through signs, jobs, institutions, food habits, fragments of conversation, bureaucratic inconveniences, local pleasures, advertisements, rituals, music, gossip, and petty disputes. Select details that characters encounter because they live there, not because the writer wants to display a world bible.

## Systems: rules, cost, and consequences

For magic, technology, law, medicine, travel, government, religion, money, and social hierarchies, distinguish rule, exception, cost, enforcement, resource limits, and narrative consequences. If a system solves today's problem, test why it could not solve yesterday's problem. Avoid inventing certainty about real law, biology, medical care, or period history.

## Logistics and geography

Record relative locations, distances, routes, travel modes/times, calendar and time of day, required preparation, arrival conditions, and local constraints. A scene may violate a travel assumption if the author invents an established shortcut; record that shortcut rather than silently teleporting characters. No map or route checker is required.

## Character-place interplay

What does a character notice that someone else ignores? How did place form assumptions, status, bodily habits, danger sense, hopes, and resentments? Change location and test what becomes impossible or newly possible in the plot.

## Discovery rather than tours

Readers often enjoy entering a functioning world mid-conversation, inferring rules from costs or misunderstandings. Reveal enough to sustain agency and comprehension. Direct exposition, a brief historical note, or an explanatory narrator is valid when chosen style and timing earn it. Do not automatically write “as you know” dialogue.

## Portable records

A location file can use: `id`, `name`, `type`, `region`, `era`, `physical texture`, `institutions`, `customs`, `culture`, `history`, `routes`, `characters`, `world rules`, `changes from chapter`, `unverified research`, `scene usages`. System records use: `governing rules`, `limits`, `costs`, `exceptions`, `observed consequences`, `date effective`. Indexes should point to one authoritative file for each world element.

## Additional international source connection

`sw-japanese-screenwriting` treats mundane material, detailed observation, diaries and place-based routines as story-generating sources. An invented fantasy world can use the same discipline: note what it costs to wash, eat, travel, repair clothing, speak to an official, wait for an answer or share a room. The contrast between the extraordinary world rule and the **ordinary life built around it** can produce comedy, romance, suspense or estrangement. Do not let world rules be an exposition lecture before characters need them.

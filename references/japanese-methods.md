# Japanese-derived writing methods adapted for novels

**Provenance:** Primarily `sources/sw/sw-japanese-screenwriting/source-skill.md` §§1-9 and its `reference.md`; relationship-writing case in `sw-series-case-studies/SKILL.md` §9 and `reference-asia.md` §9. These are descriptions of particular Japanese filmmakers and screenwriters given by the contributed skills. They are **not** claims that all Japanese authors, or all light novels, follow these conventions. This file is a selective English adaptation rather than a line-for-line translation.

## 1. Two equally legitimate starting points

| Structure-first route | Character/fragment-first route |
|---|---|
| Kosawa Ryota, Uchida Kenji, Kakei Masaya in the source | Miki Satoshi, Sono Sion, Yokohama Satoko, Takahashi Izumi, Fukuda Yuichi and Omiya Ellie in the source |
| Begin with premise, ending, a surprising "what if + moreover," goal or structural turning points | Begin with a peculiar person, lived experience, scraps of language, an image, an object, an awkward interaction or fragments that fascinate the writer |
| Plan deliberate setup and payoff; outline relational or temporal structure | Let voices and scenes expose a shape; discover theme later; assemble fragments into a coherent reading experience during revision |
| Risk: efficient but mechanical narrative | Risk: rich fragments that do not accumulate |

**Do not run both as simultaneous requirements.** Use the project choice in `story.md` or ask when designing a new project. For a discovery author, a two-sentence scene intention is optional, not a gate.

## 2. Idea generation: "if ... and moreover ..."

Kakei Masaya's source technique pairs an unlikely premise with a second independently surprising complication. Example from source categories: moving rooms plus discovering a romantic connection with a previous tenant. **Novel adaptation:** write an original sentence of the form "What if X happened, and moreover Y were true?" Then ask what ordinary person would react most interestingly to it, and which details make the world feel real. Do not confuse additive complications with arbitrary escalation.

**Miki's material notebook:** Gather observed phrases, missed connections, everyday items, gestures, apparently useless questions, odd words, and memories; group them by energy rather than by plot chronology. The source describes competing views of writing notes versus remembering jokes, so note-taking is a suggested experiment, not a command.

## 3. Character first: diaries, invisible biography, contradiction

- **Uchida:** Write a character biography and a diary for selected days occurring during the story. Find what they knew then, believed then and would refuse to admit. Use this to plant earlier foreshadowing if later choices reveal hidden motives.
- **Sono:** Resist polishing beautiful prose before a character can cause interesting consequences. A vivid secondary character may temporarily steal attention; decide whether that strengthens or derails the novel's intended ensemble.
- **Takahashi:** Write a person, not merely a job label. Use an unexpected item in their room or small behavior to counter a generic category. Ask what radiates from their humanity, not simply what plot function they serve.
- **Miki:** Create a fresh relationship pattern by introducing a third presence or an outsider; three characters can destabilize a rigid two-person opposition. This is optional, not a mandatory cast size.
- **Fukuda:** For comedy, a flaw can expose the human behind the impressive surface. Do not make flaw percentages or actor casting templates into prose rules.

## 4. Rhythm: the leap, the routine and the omission

- **Kosawa:** A sense of speed can come from jumping between contrasting events or reversing an expectation, not just from rapid action. **Prose adaptation:** cut a redundant transition and land on a meaningful new image, action or line; do not make every transition abrupt.
- **Miki / Yukisada:** The meaningful unit may be a repeated everyday pattern and the tiny deviation from it. **Prose adaptation:** repeat a morning ritual three times with one changed gesture, then let the reader infer what the relationship has become.
- **Uchida:** Work backward from a satisfying revelation to earlier evidence. Do not resolve a pivotal problem with an unprepared accident.
- **Yokohama:** Ambiguity and imbalance can be the chosen experience. The reader should be uncertain intentionally, not because geography, viewpoint or causation is accidentally confusing.
- **Kakei / Kosawa:** Select `起承転結` or another four-movement lens where useful. Do not claim it is a literal synonym for Hollywood three-act structure; the original SW text presents an approximation from one participant's practice.

## 5. Everyday scenes without forced heroism

Miki's source explicitly entertains protagonists without an explicit goal or conventional growth. Quiet fiction may be driven by attention, habit, longing, stubbornness, social friction, aesthetic interest, or small changes in perceived meaning. Do not force an adventure quest or dramatic moral conversion. A steadfast person can hold still while the reader, another character or the relationship changes. **Source conflict D3 is open for author choice.**

## 6. Dialogue emerging from different places

- **Omiya:** Interesting dialogue may begin as prolonged private self-conversation. Use this to find the line a character has rehearsed or resisted saying.
- **Fukuda:** Dialogue-first drafting can expose character and comic relationship. Let one person make a ridiculous claim and another, or the reader, supply the correction.
- **Yokohama:** Preserve the sound and ambiguity of speech. Characters may talk about facts rather than naming their emotions. Plain emotional declarations are still permitted when earned.
- **Takahashi:** A seemingly ordinary or awkward line may become the emotional climax when earlier scenes give it meaning.
- **Arai:** Writing from genuinely embarrassing or vulnerable material may create specificity. This is an invitation to authenticity, not an obligation to disclose private trauma or write autobiography.

The Korean/French counterpart `sw-korean-french-screenwriting` includes a **dialogue-last** practice. This remains an alternative composing process rather than a mandate; see `author-decisions.md` reconciled differences.

## 7. Comedy: the reader can be the straight person

The contributed Japanese skill describes comic-instigator and corrective-response roles using Chinese-language terms `逗哏` and `捧哏`, while its English description mentions audience-as-*tsukkomi*. The *boke/tsukkomi* labels here are a comparative adaptation, not a direct verbatim passage. Sometimes the work is funnier if the **reader notices the nonsense without a character spelling it out**. For a novel:

1. Establish an internally sincere, character-specific absurd claim.
2. Let context, narration or another character's reaction demonstrate its fault.
3. Choose a retort, delayed reaction, silence, narrative aside or reader-only recognition.
4. Escalate through personality and consequence, not only louder dialogue.
5. Alternate heightened comedy with plain sincerity where the story needs space.

Use these as tool options, not a claim that Japanese comedy has one exclusive form. See `comedy-engine.md` for additional sitcom-derived devices.

## 8. Original case: relationship writing through accumulation

`sw-series-case-studies` §9 describes Sakamoto Yuji's *We Made a Beautiful Bouquet* film screenplay. The supplied analysis emphasizes a relationship whose major turns are **subtle acts of consideration**, not villains or dramatic betrayals. It analyzes many brief everyday fragments punctuated by a small number of extended conversations, meaningful reuse of locations and objects, and repeated phrases that change meaning over time. **Novel adaptation:**

- Make a cafe, doorway, tabletop or mundane gift recur with a changed emotional function.
- Let a small accommodating gesture become the real pivot of the relationship.
- Time compression and changes in diary precision can communicate how memory prioritizes events.
- Give extended interior space to a few transformative encounters without mandating the source film's exact ratios or scene counts.
- Explore conflict between two sincere people whose needs gradually diverge. An antagonist is not compulsory.

**Integrity warning:** The source is a film screenplay analysis, not a Japanese light novel writing manual. The supplied file labels its inferred three-act interpretation as inference. Keep that qualification.

## 9. Scene/novel exercises

**Fragments to chapter:** Collect five vivid items or snippets. Choose two with latent emotional contradiction. Write 600 words in close third, then give it two possible interpretations: a character-first fragment and a structurally plotted seed. Do not overwrite the version whose uncertainty is intentional.

**Everyday deviation:** Write the same kitchen ritual three times separated by story time. Change only one prop, one sentence of dialogue and one internal observation. Test whether the reader can infer a developing relationship without explicit explanation.

**Reader-as-tsukkomi:** Write a short comedic exchange where no one explicitly says the ridiculous assertion is wrong. Make the reader infer the contrast from consequence; then create a second version with a corrective line and compare the effect.

**Diary to foreshadowing:** After a late-character decision, write that person's private diary for the earlier critical day, then revise an earlier prose scene with a subtle action that would become meaningful in retrospect.

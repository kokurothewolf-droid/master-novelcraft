# Prose voice, perception, rhythm and dialogue

Sources: `fiction-writing-prose(2).md`, `dialogue(1).md`, Moore P12–P17, character-distillation's speech layer.

## Narrative voice contract

Identify person (first, close-third, omniscient, multi-POV, etc.), tense, distance, diction, syntax, rhythm, vocabulary exclusions, humor tolerance, and intended readership. Voice includes **what a narrator notices**, not just their word choices. A technically “better” sentence that loses the established voice is frequently a worse edit.

For close-third, filter observation through focal character's knowledge and perception. Free indirect discourse can blend narrator and character idiom. For omniscient, allow controlled access to multiple minds through an established narrative intelligence; do not mislabel it head-hopping. In first person, differentiate sincere narrator mistakes, deliberate lies, and authorial inconsistency.

## Render and explain, in balance

Favor experience where experience matters: meaningful physical detail, gesture, time, spatial logic, texture, bodily restrictions, thought, memory, sound, and selective sense impressions. Avoid a mechanical five-senses checklist. However **summary, telling, direct thought, and authorial explanation are legitimate prose tools** for scope, tempo, comic timing, clarity and narrative voice. The source's “never explain” formulation is a strong preference, not an absolute prohibition.

For descriptions, select specific details that reveal character, place, stakes, or mood; more detail can be appropriate for wonder, ceremonial entrances, or a richly observational voice. Use crowding for density and leaping for speed deliberately. Avoid introducing every character through a repetitive dossier dump.

## Sentence craft and linguistic experimentation

Control short/long structures, sound, emphasis, paragraph breaks, punctuation, and the placement of surprising words. Specific verbs are usually stronger than weak verbs plus vague adverbs, but passive constructions, repetition, fragments, and ornament can be exactly right when intentional. Metaphors work when they clarify perception and fit the narrator's mental world. Moore's surprising juxtaposition and formal constraints are optional experiments, not a mandate for purple prose.

## Dialogue as competing action

Understand three layers **text / subtext / context**. Dialogue may reveal, evade, misdirect, assert, seduce, comfort, provoke, negotiate, or carry information. Not every line must conceal a secret; sincere literal declarations can be powerful when earned.

For two or more speaking characters, note goals, power, trust, shared history, emotional temperature, distinctive vocabularies, typical lengths, habits of interruption, strategic silence, humor, and topics avoided. Distinction can be subtle. Do **not** manufacture tics for everyone.

### Diagnostics adapted from the dialogue skill

| Symptom | Investigate | Repair options |
|---|---|---|
| D1: identical voices | Syntax/diction/directness and differing motives | Give contrasting perception and objectives, not arbitrary catchphrases |
| D2: wooden speech | Overformal complete sentences, poor turn-taking | Read aloud; selective interruption, ellipsis, rhythm variation |
| D3: exposition dump | Characters explain shared facts solely to readers | Move discovery into stakes, argument, objects or brief narration |
| D4: on-the-nose speech | Every feeling explicitly named | Introduce legitimate concealment, contradiction, behavior, or deliberate honest confrontation |
| D5: single-purpose exchange | Conversation exists only to relay plot | Layer relationship, decision, humor, setting or character revelation |
| D6: pacing mismatch | Exchange drags or outruns comprehension | Cut repeats, add purposeful beats, or create silence/physical action |

Use “said” and clear action beats when appropriate; not every dialogue tag other than “said” is bad. A character can speak directly, use formal prose, or repeat information when motivated by personality, social role, ritual, humor or conflict. Test speakers without tags when helpful, not as an absolute pass/fail.

## Line-edit anti-patterns

Watch for accidental POV access, overwritten metaphors, redundant emotion labels, monotonous rhythms, unexplained changes in object placement, unnatural performance of dialogue, exposition disguised as dialogue, narrator vocabulary leakage and too many identical beats. Each suggested change should name the *reader effect*, and an effective idiosyncrasy should be left alone.

## Expanded dialogue work from the SW archive

`sw-dialogue` describes dialogue as purposeful action, not merely delivering information: characters try to alter another person's behavior, conceal a worry, negotiate status, justify themselves, invite intimacy or resist confession. Work with **spoken text, subtext and contextual power**, but retain moments where an unadorned declaration is precisely right. A line can also be worthwhile for music, tenderness or shared ordinary life without covert chess moves.

**Japanese workflow alternatives:** Omiya Ellie's technique discovers lines from private self-conversation; Fukuda Yuichi's dialogue-first route lets a scene's voices generate a plot; Yokohama Satoko values ambiguity and the sound of speech; Takahashi Izumi builds an otherwise unremarkable phrase into a turning point through prior context. The Korean/French companion source includes a dialogue-last method. These are **separate exploratory exercises**, not stages all writers must complete.

**Reader as comic corrector:** A POV character may maintain an absurd theory while surrounding specifics quietly disprove it. The audience can supply the unspoken tsukkomi. Do not stack a correction line and a narratorial explanation unless that double acknowledgment is the intended joke.

**Emotion through narration:** Film screenwriting limits access to hidden thought; novels do not. A brief explanation of emotional reasoning can be more intimate than an elaborate gesture. Free indirect discourse should filter idiom through established consciousness, without accidental access to off-page minds.

**Voice-distinction test:** Remove names from a short exchange and see whether motive, timing, sentence patterns and relationship dynamics differentiate speakers. If not, improve more than catchphrases. Preserve unusually formal, blunt, poetic or literal speech when it belongs to a character.

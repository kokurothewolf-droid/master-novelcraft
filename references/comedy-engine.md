# Novel-native comedy engine, including full archived sitcom material

**Sources:** `sources/sw/sw-sitcom-comedy/source-skill.md` §§2-8 and its **supplied** `reference.md` §§2,6-8; standalone earlier `sw-sitcom-comedy.md` in legacy sources; Japanese `sw-japanese-screenwriting` §6. ## Comedy starts before a joke

**Premise -> predicament / character mix -> sequence -> action and dialogue -> sentence-level timing.** Ask which people reliably generate conflict simply by being themselves together. An ordinary chore can feel momentous when it threatens someone's reputation, privacy, pride, schedule, crush, or carefully maintained self-image. The humor need not erase real affection or consequence.

## Source taxonomy: nine predicament engines, plus an optional tenth

These labels and categories come from the archived reference attributed to Evan S. Smith; translation and novel use are our adaptations.

| Predicament | Prose-fiction use | What can exhaust it |
|---|---|---|
| **Big Lie** | A flimsy excuse demands increasingly expensive cover stories | Repeating the lie without changed stakes or an impending reckoning |
| **Big Secret** | Keeping one truth produces side obligations, nervous slips and new relationships | Nobody cares or the secret is already revealed |
| **Misunderstanding** | A character draws a plausible but wrong causal conclusion and acts on it | Foolish coincidences that require everyone to behave improbably |
| **Rock and Hard Place** | Two desirable options or two unacceptable obligations genuinely compete | One option is obviously harmless or clearly better |
| **Surefire Scheme** | A brilliant-to-this-person solution systematically makes the problem worse | Chaos disconnected from the person's worldview or choices |
| **Rocks the Boat** | Visitor, new policy or rival disrupts the established routine | Newcomer becomes more interesting than established people without intended refocus |
| **Time Bomb** | Deadline turns small tasks and secrecy into a cascade | Deadline has no consequences |
| **Trapped in a Space** | Unsuitable companions share a carriage, room, inn bath or tiny kitchen | Geography is vague, they could leave easily, or nothing evolves |
| **Sex / romantic entanglement** | Attraction, embarrassment, privacy, jealousy and conflicting intimacy needs | Treating endless denial of consensual relationships as the sole engine |
| **Optional: Dead Body / morbid premise** | Dark or absurd comedy where danger and moral tone are explicitly contracted | Reader genuinely fears gratuitous harm while prose insists the event is harmless |

Choose only fitting categories, never checklist-fill them. The archived reference identifies the dead-body example as a **tenth**, although it initially refers to nine classic predicament types. Do not misleadingly call this a strict nine-item exhaustive system.

## Source taxonomy: five operational character mixes

- **Role reversal:** an orderly person must improvise, a confident host must be a guest, a protector must accept protection.
- **Fish out of water:** a worldview is tested by unfamiliar customs. The joke is in incompatible expectations, not a foreign person's accent or identity.
- **Odd couple:** different social or practical systems are forced into shared space.
- **Romantic tension:** two people desire intimacy but have asymmetrical information, priorities, courage or timing. Long novels can allow actual relationship development.
- **Pretense:** someone must maintain an image they cannot sustain. An amusing reveal can expose a blind spot, not necessarily humiliate them.

The archived source contains a dated sixth marketing observation about identity representation. It does not provide a useful craft mechanism and is **not activated** as a novel formula. Avoid stereotypes and treating identities as punchlines.

## Sentence-level tools

**Setup:** Orient the reader toward a believable interpretation and add enough tension, detail or expectation that a reversal matters. **Turn/punchline:** Place the key pivot where it lands with maximum clarity, often near the end of a sentence or short paragraph. **Topper:** A second surprise can build energy, but it can also trample the first or sabotage emotion. **Callback:** Return to a line, prop, social rule, gesture or interrupted task in a way that **changes meaning**. **Running gag:** Familiar setup plus fresh variation. **Awkward silence:** The reader may supply a correction nobody has the courage to say.

Character-specific expression can include understatement, overstatement, misplaced confidence, dry irony, literalism, evasive chatter, bravado, overprecision, or playful verbal ambiguity. Don't assign every character one fixed gimmick.

## Japanese adjacent comedy: boke / tsukkomi / reader inference

The Japanese screenwriting skill discusses comic-instigator and corrective-response roles (`逗哏` / `捧哏` in its Chinese text), and its English description mentions an audience-as-*tsukkomi* technique. The *boke/tsukkomi* terminology here is a comparative prose adaptation. In fiction, the reader can temporarily occupy the corrective role while every character continues with astonishing sincerity. This can be stronger than inserting a character who announces "That makes no sense" after every line. Alternatively, give the response to a second person whose manner and stakes shape the retort. Let both roles swap as relationships evolve.

**Novel expansion:** The narrator's internal monologue can supply a delayed realization. Precise timing may depend on paragraph breaks, a gesture, a single intervening sentence, or free indirect thought. No camera and no stage direction are necessary.

## Dramedy and relief

Do not puncture all vulnerability with a quip. Characters can be funny because they are emotionally honest, desperately evasive, or unexpectedly kind. A comedy can build toward a serious confession; a tragedy can admit an absurd detail. Choose where the story lets the reader breathe. Moore's observations about horror and humor's shared surprise mechanics are an analytical connection, not an instruction to mix both in every passage.

## Comedy in a recurring inn, household, school, guild or party

Keep a durable **comic engine**: repeated proximity, competing rules, unequal information, incompatible bodies/abilities, recurring shared tasks, social hierarchies and emotional agendas. Across chapters, change context, power balance and fallout. Unlike a traditional sitcom, a novel should track real consequences if the author wants cumulative relationships.

## Five-step comedy revision

1. Identify the **specific character intention** behind the attempted joke.
2. Determine whether premise, situation or dialogue is carrying it; rewrite upstream when necessary.
3. Check if the reader has enough orientation to perceive incongruity.
4. Improve placement and rhythm without sacrificing narrator authenticity.
5. Preserve plot causality, tone and emotional aftermath; remove jokes that flatten important moments.

**Excluded:** sitcom script layout, laugh/page counts, advertising act breaks, fixed episode beat quotas, production standing sets, obligatory romantic delay and status-quo resets. Case-specific numbers may be studied as historical examples, not light novel metrics.

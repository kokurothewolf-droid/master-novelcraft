# Author decision register: genuine disagreements

**Status:** OPEN for author preference. No source winner has been declared. This file is not a command to choose on the author's behalf. The active skill can perform individual tasks without these decisions by following the explicit request and existing project style.

## D1. When is theme fixed?

- **A. Theme-first.** Decide a premise or central thematic claim before substantial drafting; use it to select events and determine the ending. Supported by `sw-premise-theme/SKILL.md` §§1-2,5 and `fiction-writing-story-development(1).md` §1.
- **B. Theme-discovery.** Draft from characters, fragments or situations; let theme emerge during writing or revision, then shape the manuscript around what appears. Supported by `sw-japanese-screenwriting/SKILL.md` §§1,3 (Miki Satoshi and others); `sw-series-case-studies/SKILL.md` §2 describes retrospective theme in *The Sopranos*.
- **C. Project-by-project.** Ask for a selection when starting a book, permit changes as the author's method develops, and record whether the theme is provisional or approved.

**Why substantive:** A skill cannot simultaneously require the writer to settle an immutable theme before writing and require it to emerge only after drafting. **Current neutral behavior:** Treat theme as a hypothesis unless author-established; do not prevent discovery drafts.

## D2. How much outlining should the master expect?

- **A. Structure-first.** Premise, book spine and major turning points established before chapter drafting. Draw on `sw-story-structure`, `sw-truby-anatomy`, `plot-structure(1).md`, and the Kosawa/Uchida/Kakei section of `sw-japanese-screenwriting`.
- **B. Character/fragment-first.** Build from observed moments, competing character agendas, voices and scenes; organize or re-architect after the material grows. Draw on Miki, Yokohama, Sono, Takahashi and Fukuda in `sw-japanese-screenwriting` and the on-the-fly case-study note in `sw-series-case-studies`.
- **C. Hybrid.** Keep a provisional endpoint or dramatic promise and a flexible relationship/volume map; discovery-draft scenes and revise the outline after major revelations.

**Why substantive:** A mandatory full beat sheet before drafting is incompatible with deliberately avoiding a beat sheet to discover story. **Current neutral behavior:** No mandatory beat-sheet gate; user can request either.

## D3. Are explicit protagonist goals and transformation required?

- **A. Strong dramatic spine.** A leading character should actively want something consequential, face escalating resistance and undergo significant change. Supported by `sw-character-conflict` §2, `fiction-writing-story-development(1).md` §2 and the author's additional point 1.
- **B. Flexible character-state model.** The character may have a small, unspoken or short-term concern; an episodic, slice-of-life or steadfast character can remain substantially the same. Supported by `sw-japanese-screenwriting` §2 (Miki), episodic comedy techniques, and flat-arc models in `fiction-writing-story-development(1).md`.
- **C. Contract-dependent.** Decide per book/volume/POV character; maintain a story-level effect even when the individual doesn't change.

**Why substantive:** “Every protagonist must change” contradicts “a protagonist can remain unchanged.” **Current neutral behavior:** Do not force external quests or growth; investigate desire, attention, decisions, change in relationships, or shift in the reader's understanding.

## Reconciled by form or reader effect, not awaiting author votes

| Apparent opposition | Noncontradictory implementation |
|---|---|
| Film/TV scene must contain action/value reversal vs contemplative prose | Test for *purpose* rather than mandatory visible action; document whether purpose is emotional, atmospheric, comic or structural. |
| “Never explain” vs worldbuilding clarity and narrator commentary | Selective exposition, summary, omniscient interpretation and interior monologue are allowed when they serve voice and orientation. |
| “Start at peak” vs slow atmospheric openings | Choose by desired narrative promise, not a universal start-late mandate. |
| Literal “be a sadist” vs cozy/romantic tone | Meaningful pressure may be slight, relational or funny. No arbitrary cruelty requirement. |
| Genre checklist completeness vs originality | Reader promise informs expectations; deliberate omissions/reinterpretations are valid if intentional. |
| Sitcom reset/status quo vs multi-volume consequences | Retain repeatable character engines while tracking cumulative relationship and world changes. |
| Precise numeric film beat/page models vs prose pacing | Screenplay numbers are case evidence only, not novel quotas. |
| Japanese `起承転結` / Chinese `起承转合` vs three acts | Related structural lenses, not mathematically interchangeable universal models. Do not claim strict equivalence. |
| Character traits as immutable prediction vs long-term development | Trait profile applies at story time; scene evidence, turning points and author decisions can legitimately update it. |
| Write dialogue first (Fukuda) vs dialogue last (Auburtin) | Both are compositional experiments. Pick according to author workflow; neither is a manuscript-wide order requirement. |
| Moore-style linguistic adventure vs economical transparent prose | Use experimentation sparingly to serve narrator and effect; don't decorate every line. |

## Decision form for an author

The user may choose D1 (A/B/C), D2 (A/B/C), and D3 (A/B/C) as project-wide defaults. Until then, this release is a **consistent, executable release candidate** with no conflicting universal requirements. Record a confirmed selection in the specific novel's `story.md`, not in raw source documents. A future version can record personal global defaults if the user requests them.

We have been discussing character, but it is important to remember that any character, you and me included, is inevitably a product of the place and time from which we originated. Let us discuss place first. Place is incredibly important to a story and to a writer; to me, it has recently become almost the most important thing. An understanding of place will greatly help your story and allow you to understand where your characters are coming from psychologically, as the world in which they spend their lives inevitably has its effects upon them.

If you are writing about a real place, the method for investigating it might be a bit complex and time-consuming, but it certainly pays off through a practice akin to psychogeography. I recall that when I was starting the *Swamp Thing* series, one of the first questions I asked the comic company was, "Which swamp is he the thing of?" They looked at me blankly, as they had not really considered location to be a major factor, suggesting it might be the Florida Everglades or Louisiana. I chose Louisiana because I liked the music. I started to research the place, gathering a local phrasebook, a book of local slang, maps of the state and individual towns, and a record of jukebox hits to understand the musical culture. I read about its history, the plants that grow there (like Spanish moss and water hyacinths), and the local birds, such as blue herons. You do not need very much, and you will already know more about the place than most of the people actually living there.

Most people have no awareness at all of the landscapes in which they are living. If they had more awareness, I suspect they might enjoy those places more. If we walk down a street and all we see are gray terraces devoid of any meaning, we might start to think we are living in a bit of a rat trap. Do that long enough, and you will think, "I'm living in a rat trap. I'm probably a rat, aren't I?" It soaks in because, to some degree, we are the places we live. Conversely, if you are walking down a street and you know its history and all the rich circumstances that led up to it, it will be like walking down a street from the *Arabian Nights*. It becomes a fantastic avenue of glory and mythology. You will eventually think, "I'm living in a mythological landscape. Maybe I'm a mythological being." It works both ways: the places we live can degrade us and make us feel like empty, useless individuals in an empty landscape, or they can elevate us. Find out about places and make them burn with meaning and significance again, and you will find that the people living in them will burn with meaning and significance as well.

We have talked about how to represent a real location, but you will almost certainly have to create a place that is not real at some point. Creating a world is a challenge, but it is not beyond your capabilities. Just remember that the world you are creating should have internal consistency. It does not matter whether it is nothing like our world at all or has different physical rules, as long as those rules are self-consistent. Whether you are talking about a world like Mervyn Peake's *Gormenghast*, Ursula K. Le Guin's *Earthsea*, or the fabulously, horrifically overgrown Eden of Brian Catling's extraordinary *Vorrh* trilogy—where a forest has somehow spread through space and time to exist in every conceivable country and different time periods at once—you can keep even the most fantastic imagination consistent. One way to create an imaginary world is by considering its texture, as this is one of the first things a reader will look for to make it feel real. By "texture," I mean all the trappings we find around us in society that we barely notice: advertisements, scraps of news broadcasts, television programs, and snippets of songs on the radio. If you want to create an imaginary place, try faking some of that texture.

While it is immensely important to get the place right, it is possibly even more important to ensure you are not making any mistakes regarding the period. This entails quite a lot of research. To a certain degree, if you can remember a period, you can use that to enhance your depiction of it. I can remember the 1970s, the 1960s, and a bit of the 1950s, so I would use that to color my impressions. However, I do not really know much about those eras, so I would still have to research them. We actually miss quite a lot of what is going on in the eras we live through; we do not necessarily understand the times we are living in. In fact, it can be much more revealing to look back with hindsight from a later date when we have all the information. If there is any way you can find out details, do it, so that you know exactly what streets your characters would have to walk down to get from one place to another. This gives incredible confidence and authenticity to your writing, which conveys itself to the reader. They will think, "This person sounds like they know what they are talking about," and they will trust you because there is something about authentic detail that really cannot be imitated. You know it when you hear it.

When I was working on the *Halo Jones* comic strip, I wanted to immerse readers in the reality of the 50th-century world I was imagining. What I did was drop the readers *in media res*—right in the middle of everything without explaining it. They arrive in the story halfway through a news broadcast that is talking about unfathomable things using unfathomable slang, mentioning celebrities like Algie, Baron Lux, Roth, or Chop, whom the reader has never heard of. This gives the reader the sense of being dumped into a real place and having to figure it all out, which is actually quite a satisfying experience. It is not necessarily alienating, because if readers are given a chance to piece things together, they will delight in doing so. It is what human beings do; we like to figure things out.

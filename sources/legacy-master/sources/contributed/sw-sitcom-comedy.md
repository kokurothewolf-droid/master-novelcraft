# Sitcom and Half-Hour Comedy

Respond in the user's language. Retain established industry terms rather than inventing new translations.

Build comedy into the premise before adding jokes to dialogue. Work through three levels: **premise → sequence/scene → dialogue/action**. Ask first whether the premise itself can keep producing jokes. Humor planted upstream should generate stronger, more integrated laughs downstream.

Treat a half-hour as a different writing system from an hour drama: change page assumptions, formatting, scene length, laugh density, structural units, and relationship dynamics together.

## 1. Switching from hour drama to half-hour comedy

| Dimension | Half-hour working model | Hour-drama comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Script length | Single-camera/film: 27–30 pages; multi-camera/tape: 45–50; animation: 40–50 | 48–63 pages in the cited model |
| Acts | Cold open + two acts for many multi-camera shows; two to four for single-camera; tag | Teaser + four, five, or six acts |
| Two-act timing | Teaser 30–60 seconds; each act 10–12 minutes; tag no longer than a minute | Acts roughly 10–15 minutes |
| Three-act first act | 1–5 pages in Smith's 30-page, single-main-story, established-world model | Roughly one-quarter |
| Scenes | Multi-camera: cited total 15–20 scenes, often long; cited per-act range 3–5, dependent on act count. Single-camera: more, shorter scenes; HIMYM cited at 40–50, sometimes 80 | Format-dependent |
| Standing sets | Three ideal, five as a cited budget ceiling | Generally more locations |
| Structural units | Main plot 5–9 beats; subplot 3–5; equal-weight threads 3–6 each | Scenes and act outs |
| Laugh density | Smith's target: 2–4 actual laughs per formatted page | Humor remains useful without that quota |
| Stakes | A small matter of enormous personal importance: the tremendous trifle | Often life, career, or institutional stakes |
| Silence | Multi-camera silence may feel like dead air; single-camera silence can sustain cringe | Silence is a dramatic resource |
| Growth | Traditional sitcoms often expand a stable premise and partly reset | More sustained serialized change |

Do not combine incompatible structural models. Apply section 4 before using act-length figures. Treat the scene-total and per-act figures as separate planning observations, not an arithmetic mandate.

Build the engine from dense relationships. Recurring proximity and intimacy keep conflict productive even without a major inciting event.

Write multi-camera as sustained shared-space action: fewer, longer scenes, with motivated entrances and exits. Write single-camera with greater freedom to cut, interweave short scenes, and use visual comedy. A change of layout alone does not change the form. Tailor a spec sample to the form being sought.

## 2. Premise-driven comedy: three levels

### Level one: premise

Weave funny elements into the premise, exploit and compound them in sequences, then grow dialogue and action from the humor already present. Build rather than repair.

Consider four sources: **predicaments, character mix, style of comedy, and casting**.

Distinguish a scene predicament that lasts a few pages from a continuing predicament that generates jokes until resolved. An unresolved problem creates anticipation that can strengthen even unrelated laughs. Mine a series' permanent predicament for episode ideas. Establish the intended comic contract, including how seriously harm should be understood.

Treat each show as its own genre. Two domestic comedies can create radically different audience expectations. Before writing an existing show, identify:

- Whether humor comes mainly from predicaments, relationships, or environment.
- Whether the desired response is a belly laugh or a knowing smile.
- Whether comedy is primarily visual or verbal.
- Whether an episode builds to a concentrated comic run or distributes laughs evenly.

Reject **Too Funny to Pass Up**: a funny idea that does not belong in this show. Avoid **The Genre Shuffle**: abruptly changing the audience contract rather than consistently blending genres. Expand the show's premise without accidentally replacing it.

### Level two: sequences and scenes

Treat a sequence as connected scenes sustaining one action; change scenes when place or time changes.

1. Compound predicaments within an unfolding passage. Escalate a lie into larger lies, or combine different problems. Let each complication cause the next rather than merely repeat it.
2. Stir the character mix through an unnatural alliance. Let viewers anticipate both the problem's resolution and the alliance's collapse; restore or deliberately revise the relationship afterward.
3. Escalate toward a comic climax. Mine the regular characters' foibles and check consistency of comic intensity before polishing dialogue.

Give comic characters a weak self-editing mechanism: impulsive choices and disproportionate reactions to problems others might shrug off. Make those choices specific to the character.

## 3. Joke mechanics: level three

Use joke terminology primarily to edit and repair. A setup establishes a subject and a complication or incongruity, creating tension. A punchline resolves it through an unexpected but intelligible turn.

### Setups

1. Begin with something ordinary and believable. A conspicuously strange setup can advertise the trap.
2. Include both the subject and one or two beats of development. Without development, the punchline may register as extra information. Development also controls hostility, warmth, and fidelity to character.
3. Distribute setup elements through a scene or plant them in earlier scenes when useful.
4. Remove wording that reveals the punchline. An unnecessary question can destroy surprise.
5. Use shared everyday frustrations or cultural knowledge as an implicit first half of the setup when the audience will recognize them.

### Punchlines

1. Put the punch word last. Order the sentence so the decisive reinterpretation lands at its end.
2. Stop after the joke. Additional dialogue often steps on the laugh, unless rambling itself is the character's established rhythm.
3. Use a **topper** for a second or third escalating punchline from one setup. Smith's working limit is two tops after the original punchline. Leave some jokes alone to protect pacing or dramatic weight; usually avoid toppers in dramedy.
4. Return to a **running gag** two or three times with new meaning. A callback supplies a new punchline referring to an earlier joke. Change the situation, speaker, or setup; often reverse the meaning on its final return. Distinguish a joke runner from a runner meaning a small subplot.
5. When a punchline resists repair, change the setup's incongruity or contrast.
6. Try **Go for Literal**: interpret the expression literally. Make this fit the character, often someone exceptionally foolish or exceptionally clever.

### Character-specific comic nuances

Give each character one or two characteristic comic responses before drafting dialogue. The supplied list includes Understated Response, Exaggerated Response, Irony, Nervous Babbling, Pulls No Punches, Space-Shot POV, Wordplay, and Double Entendre. Two promised emotional variants were not supplied. Do not claim a complete ten-item taxonomy.

Use these as character habits, not interchangeable author tricks. Check whether speakers remain recognizable with their names hidden.

### General principles

- **Hang a lantern:** let a character acknowledge an implausibility when that helps the audience accept it. Foreshadow improbable developments and pay off planted seeds.
- **Think bigger:** increase exposure, audience, or consequences when consistent with character and premise.
- **Small truths, big laughs:** ground exaggeration in recognizable frustration and behavior.
- Apply Reiss's three tests: funny, useful to the story, and compatible with the audience's relationship to the character. Do not solve a difficult slot with a joke that undermines the whole scene.

### Density

Count actual comic moments rather than estimating. For joke-driven sitcoms, use the cited 2–4 laughs per formatted page as a diagnostic target, including the opening pages. Do not assume a pilot earns several unfunny pages of setup.

Distinguish smile, chuckle, and laugh. Visual or physical comedy may produce a different response from witty dialogue. Keep the quantity and kind of humor consistent with the show's contract. Use toppers and running gags economically without becoming excessively jokey. Do not impose this density target on dramedy or unformatted text.

## 4. Half-hour structure

### Beat sheet

Start with one page. Use 5–9 beats for the main plot, 3–5 for each subplot, or 3–6 for each equal-weight thread in a Seinfeld-style structure. Define a beat as a discovery, event, or moment that changes the central character's goal or raises tension, often action followed by reaction.

Write each beat as one sentence beginning with its central character's name. Rewrite external events as that character's response. Outline separate threads before weaving them together. Check escalation, logic, comedy, and surprise. Note that drama rooms may use beat sheet to mean a longer numbered scene outline.

### Three-act, single-main-story model

Move from a new problem or opportunity to an unreasonable pursuit, escalating obstacles, and finally exposure, embarrassment, confrontation, or partial recognition.

- Act one: introduce the inciting incident within one or two beats.
- Act two: develop rather than repeat; occupy roughly one-half to two-thirds, often ending in a major revelation and choice.
- Act three: accelerate toward the climax, truth, and resolution; roughly one-quarter.

For Smith's 30-page film-format model, use **1–5 / 15–20 / 5–10 pages** as flexible ranges, selecting allocations that fit the total. Equal-weight four-thread stories may allot roughly 7–8 pages per thread without predictable act-break pages.

**Choose the act scheme before diagnosing length:**

- Two-act model: a cold open, two acts of roughly 10–12 minutes each, and a tag. The first act is not exceptionally short.
- Three-act, established-world, single-main-story model: the short 1–5-page first act can apply.
- Pilot: world and character introductions may require a longer first act. Do not fail it against the established-world rule.

### Dramatic structure versus broadcast format

Distinguish a dramatic act from the segment between commercial breaks. Their boundaries need not coincide. Script labels such as ACT ONE follow the production's broadcast format.

Build the beat sheet first. In the outline, move important plot turns toward the show's established commercial breaks where possible. Write strong act outs that create a reason to return.

### Key dramatic moment and block comedy scene

Identify the episode's central decision, discovery, or reconciliation before locating it in a scene. Matt Williams's cited model places it in the major block comedy scene most of the time. Try making the largest comic passage also deliver the key emotional or dramatic turn.

### Six-episode seasons

For a single-author streaming half-hour, consider cold open → titles → sequences → button. Pay off the season's theme in the finale button. A recurring object that changes hands can connect episodes. Do not present these suggestions as the missing full Fleabag episode template.

## 5. Characters worth putting in the machine

Give characters drive, purpose, vulnerability, and a distinctive competence or spark of passion. They need not be conventionally likable. A troublemaker can remain compelling through capability, desire, or failed good intentions.

Build a recurring reason to bring regulars together. Proximity need not mean one living room or office, but the engine must keep generating encounters. Prefer conflict between regulars over stories driven entirely by visiting strangers.

Use complementary traits: a hospital, restaurant, apartment, or incubator is not inherently funny; its people generate comedy. For an ensemble, try describing each person positively, making any pair productive, and giving each viewpoint some validity.

Treat these ten behavioral roles as temporary modes, not permanent labels: Supportive Parent, Idiot Savant, Idiot Idiot, Clown, Operator, Mentor, Confidant, Irritant, Romantic Interest, Critic. Let one character behave differently across relationships and episodes.

For a Fleabag-style naming device, reduce supporting characters to one stable characteristic only when that reduction expresses the protagonist's own gift or flaw. Eventually make that habit cost them something; introduce someone they cannot easily label.

## 7. Direct address and device-driven comedy

Treat direct address as a character's psychological defense with dramatic consequences. Use narrated setup, an aside inside an exchange, or a silent glance. Ask what the character gains by performing for the audience.

1. Test the device before committing to a script. A five-minute phone recording of a performed scene can establish whether it works.
2. Establish a pattern, then let another character overturn it. An impressive adjective followed by unimpressive evidence can expose the speaker. Give the reversal to the opponent rather than always to the protagonist.
3. Contrast jokes to the audience with truth told to another person. In a Fleabag-like design, reserving an unmediated monologue can make it an emotional peak; do not treat that specific count as a universal rule.
4. Give the device an arc. It may begin as complicity, become an intrusive witness, be noticed by another character, be resisted, and finally be dismissed. Resolve the device when its psychological job ends.
5. Write visual sentences: a brief, concrete image of trauma can intrude when the protagonist appears happy. Let the image reveal distress without explanatory speech.

Try **pain → laugh → pain** for an emotional scene: truth, a visual joke or cutting line, then truth again. Let the protagonist lose an argument when a prop or action exposes their position; allow another character to deepen the theme.

For a Fleabag-inspired finale, consider an anticlimactic progression: declaration, gentle rejection, reciprocal acknowledgment, an everyday setback, a comic prop, an object linked to the dead, and farewell to the device. Preserve the underlying emotional logic instead of copying a fixed beat count or line length.

## 8. Dramedy and comedy carrying tragedy

Build dramedy from continuing characters and stories, substantial backstory, and dramatic arcs. Let humor be wry or ironic rather than requiring a setup/punchline at fixed intervals. Sustain story beyond joke-to-joke construction. Establish audience expectations through tone and form; the upload's Frank's Place example warns against promising one experience and delivering another.

Use the supplied Succession observations:

- Follow a dark confession with jokes when that family's cruel humor is its available language of acceptance. Let the character relationship justify the response.
- Make a comic subplot regulate temperature **and** perform a plot function. Greg's Luxembourg succession material is the cited example of apparently light material affecting Tom's decisions.
- Consider a cooperative, regressive game before a finale rupture. Humor can briefly unite the ensemble; an interrupted attempt at tenderness can foreshadow the ending. An emotional or relational function can justify a scene without advancing external plot.
- Prefer proposals grounded in reality over mythic abstraction, occasionally yielding to an especially strong comic choice.

## 9. Sitcom pilots and series formats

Consider a typical-episode pilot when it can demonstrate the repeatable show. A premise pilot dramatizes the event that creates the series, but must still show what future episodes will be like. Treat the upload's claim about executive preferences as historical advice, not current market research.

Make the opening funny, count its laughs, prioritize compelling characters, establish proximity, keep the story simple, and delay exposition where possible. Clarify ownership and adaptation rights when relevant, using current authoritative guidance rather than assuming historical submission advice is law.

For the cited 10–15-page series-format model, demonstrate **legs**: a premise capable of generating many episodes. Introduce characters briefly in the premise section, then let action and dialogue define them. Make the document itself a sample of the show's comic voice. The upload's complete eleven-section template was not supplied.

For episode pitching, the cited preparation model is 10–15 springboards of 3–5 sentences each, beginning with the short version. For a new-show pitch, lead with one central idea and one or two backups; stop pitching backups once the central idea is accepted. Make the pitch language funny.

## 10. The comedy room

Use a common sequence: collective story breaking → individual first draft → collective punch-up. Roundtable writing may involve 5–15 people rewriting line by line under a senior decision-maker. Expect substantial rewriting without treating every room's workflow as identical.

Create **alts**, alternate lines or jokes, and save usable discarded jokes in a **candy bag**. Use the strongest structural ideas now rather than hoarding them. Schur's cited process can expand a script to 50–60 pages with alts before cutting back to 30; use this as an example, not a requirement.

For a focused alts pass, identify up to 15 lines, generate 10–20 alternatives, and keep 5–10 strongest options where useful. The supplied on-set model circulates these shortly before filming and offers selected alternatives after the scripted take.

| Room example | Supplied method | Useful consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Parks and Recreation / Schur | Break together, draft individually; avoid diminishing returns from writing every line collectively | Protect drafting momentum |
| Mindy Kaling | Cards and whiteboard; pitch several story-area documents; assign one or two first-draft writers, then share ownership | Preserve a patchwork of voices |
| Insecure / Issa Rae | Write episodes collectively; writers and producers read roles aloud before the actor table read | Test a unified voice aloud |
| Schitt's Creek / Dan Levy | Four to six writers work closely with strict A/B/C stories; turn beats into dialogue before assignments | Protect tonal precision |

Do not leave beats at the level of someone being caught and escaping embarrassment. Specify the embarrassment, its character function, and whether the physical comedy also carries emotion. Draft the teaser's dialogue early to test whether the beats actually play.

Consider preserving an individual writer's ownership of intimate dramatic passages even when comic material receives room-wide punch-up. Treat this as a craft choice, not a ban on collaborative drama.

## 12. Diagnostic checklist

### Premise

- Can the premise generate jokes without forcing wordplay into dialogue?
- Does the predicament persist long enough, and when does it resolve?
- Do two characters contribute the same comic function?
- Does the episode expand the show's premise while preserving its contract?
- Have an irresistible stray joke or a sudden genre shift displaced the show?
- What repeatedly brings the regulars together?

### Structure and production

- Does each beat alter a goal or raise tension? Do the chosen beat ranges help?
- Apply the short-first-act check **only** to the three-act, single-main-story, non-pilot model.
- Locate the key dramatic moment and its relationship to the largest comic passage.
- Check escalation, distinct thread leads, protagonist agency, act outs, late scene entries, early exits, and buttons.
- Count standing sets for multi-camera; count locations and company moves for single-camera.

### Jokes

- Count actual laughs, including the first three formatted pages, where the sitcom-density target applies.
- Check setup development, concealed punchlines, final punch words, and clean stops.
- Check overused toppers, inappropriate toppers in dramedy, repeated gags without new meaning, and unpaid plants.
- Repair stubborn punchlines upstream or try a literal interpretation.
- Test humor, story function, and character effect together.
- Check distinct comic voices and consistent density/type.
- Replace unnecessary spoken jokes with reactions, stage business, visual humor, or sound when those play better.

### Format and tone

- Match the intended show's script format and distinguish submission from production conventions.
- Check parenthetical excess and unsupported production notation.
- In dramedy, preserve wry or ironic humor without converting every exchange to joke delivery.
- Check whether comic subplots also affect plot, emotion, theme, or relationships.
- Prefer beats that perform more than one useful function; revise single-purpose material before automatically cutting a necessary transition or quiet moment.

## 13. Workflow: idea to second draft

0. **Select and study a show for a spec.** Choose one you understand and enjoy. Read actual scripts, not only episodes. Map scene-start page, a one-line scene description, A/B/C beats, and the last scene before each commercial break. Treat historical advice about which shows to submit elsewhere as submission-specific.
1. **Generate ideas from emotion and goals.** Ask what-if questions that force conflicting regulars together. Evaluate topical shelf life; avoid depending on celebrity cameos or holiday exceptions unless commissioned.
2. **Write a 3–5-sentence springboard.** Establish the hook, then imply funny complications. A pitch need not always disclose the ending. Write it with comic energy; if the essence cannot be explained briefly, clarify the concept.
3. **Build the one-page beat sheet.** Outline threads separately, then interweave. Test build, logic, surprise, and humor.
4. **Repair comedy at premise level.** Identify the predicament, the moment the character mix changes, and the show's style. Do not proceed until those are clear.
5. **Write the outline.** The supplied models are 5–7 single-spaced pages or 8–12 double-spaced, subject to room practice. Include only information needed to follow the story. Hook at least one thread within two or three pages. Describe actual playable comedy rather than promising a scene will be funny. Use only brief, excellent dialogue samples. Arrange strong turns before broadcast breaks. Inspect the two-thirds point and ask what the episode is about.
6. **Draft without polishing page one indefinitely.** The source's example pace is one to two weeks for a 30-page draft. Write through scenes; return to beats and outline before major restructuring. Count laughs when the draft is complete.
7. **Revise in order: story → sequences → scenes → moments → dialogue.** Examine comedy at every level. Listen through feedback before defending choices. Trace vague notes upstream: an unfunny scene may lack a predicament established several scenes earlier. Make the second draft materially respond to agreed notes.

## 14. Practice

1. Count and classify laughs in a produced script: setup/punchline, topper, running gag, reaction, stage business, visual, or sound. Repeat on the current draft.
2. Select three weak punchlines. Change only their setups first, then see whether each revised setup generates six fresh punchlines.
3. Test an unconventional device with a five-minute performed phone video before developing a full script.
4. Generate 10–20 alts for a scene, select 5–10 useful options, and retain viable discards in the candy bag.


Sometime during your career as a writer, you will frequently find yourself writing within a specific genre. My advice is to choose a genre you already genuinely enjoy and are excited about before plunging into its fertile undergrowth. Interestingly, much of modern genre fiction traces its roots back to Northampton, specifically to the graveyard poetry movement, which grew out of the writings of the miserable but eloquent Northampton Divine, James Hervey. Hervey wrote incredibly morbid but beautifully worded pieces about funeral processions and the decay of the flesh, emphasizing that only God is eternal. The first graveyard poets, such as Thomas Gray in his *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard*, adopted these themes, using skulls, bats, and tombstones as symbols of the material world falling away to reveal immortal glory. Eventually, "lighter" graveyard poets emerged who cared little for the religious aspect but absolutely loved the ghoulish elements. Writers like Horace Walpole read these poems and wondered if a novel could be built around this macabre atmosphere, thus inventing the Gothic novel. This scandalized the literary establishment of the time, which believed that only the polite "comedy of manners" (like the works of Sheridan or Jane Austen) constituted proper literature. Despite being historically excluded beyond the security fence of "high" literature, genre is actually where literature gets most of its energy and vitality.

Working in a genre is a heady experience, but you should always choose one you know well. If you write crime fiction, for example, you will be intimately acquainted with its clichés: the world-weary private eye, the heart-stopping but treacherous blonde who draws him into a web of deceit, and so on. Because you know these tropes, you can play against them, bend them, or break them entirely. As you mature as a writer, you might want to move beyond strict genre boundaries and mash them together, much like real life. In reality, we are not neatly separated into tragedies, comedies, romances, or science fiction dramas; they are all happening simultaneously every day. We live in a soap opera that can instantly pivot into a mystery, a hardboiled crime story, or a sci-fi narrative. Smashing down the walls between genres provides an incredible release of energy that can fuel your stories.

An important consideration, perhaps as crucial as choosing a genre, is deciding the mode in which to tell your story. On one level, this is the choice between comedy and drama. Almost any story you have ever heard could have been told in the opposite mode; with a slight shift in emphasis, the most horrific or dramatic events could easily become comedies. Comedy and horror actually operate on the exact same principle: they both set up a specific expectation and then deliberately betray it. Consider the classic Tommy Cooper joke: "A man walks into a bar, he breaks his arm." The joke sets up the expectation of a drinking establishment, then subverts it with the physical object of a bar, resulting in a comic betrayal of expectations. Horror narratives do the exact same thing, but the surprise is startling and frightening rather than humorous. The two modes are so closely linked that laughter is a common, natural tension-release for audiences watching horror films, and an effect can sometimes be so over-the-top that laughter is the only possible reaction.

On a more technical level, you must decide on the tense and the point of view. Your main choices for tense are past and present (I do not advise the future tense). Past tense is the most commonly used; it is comfortable to the ear, non-threatening, and provides a sense of historical distance. Present tense, conversely, offers much more immediacy and engagement, making the narrative feel electric and unfolding right now. Regarding point of view, the third person ("he," "she," "it") is broadly useful because it is omniscient. A third-person narrator already knows everything and can jump across locations and minds without being restricted to a single character's knowledge. First person, however, is highly immediate and powerful, putting the reader directly inside the character's head. While more difficult to sustain, it allows for marvelous literary tricks, such as the unreliable narrator. The crime writer Jim Thompson was a master of this. In one of his books, a first-person narrator who is a law officer slowly reveals himself over ten pages to be a psychopathic monster who despises everyone. In another, a narrator describes a brutal act of domestic abuse, only to recount the same event chapters later as a good-natured, amusing accident. The reader realizes the narrator is lying to them, and more disturbingly, to himself, editing his own past. This provides a fantastic, creepy insight into the character's psyche.

There is also a highly useful third alternative: the close third person. In this mode, you write in the third person, but you stay so close to the character that you can hear their internal thoughts. For example, in the second chapter of my novel *Jerusalem*, the narrative follows a crack-addicted sex worker named Mara. Though it is written in the third person, we are so close to her that we experience her thoughts looping like a rat maze from need to fear to anger. We hear her made-up internal arguments, shouting in block capital letters at a mother who isn't in the room and whom she hasn't seen in three years. This approach gives you the best of both worlds: deep intimacy with the character's experience, combined with the ability to pull back and observe them from the outside. This technique facilitates "free indirect discourse," a term borrowed from narratology that allows the reader to dip in and out of different characters' consciousnesses. This profoundly increases the reader's empathy, enabling them to see inside even flawed or villainous characters and understand why they do what they do. Ultimately, your choices of person and tense should be carefully considered based on the specific effect you want to have on your reader.


You can imagine a whole range of alternate personalities for yourself, inhabiting your characters rather than seeing them as separate people. Ideally, all the characters in your story are extensions of yourself, much like how all the characters in our dreams are us. You must inhabit these characters with conviction to convince your readers that they are real. Of course, if you are writing about a genuine historical individual, the process is slightly different. It still requires empathy and understanding, but you will accomplish most of it through research. This is a good place to mention the importance of research and the need to come to love it. Don't think of it as homework or just being surrounded by dusty books; if you can learn to enjoy expanding your knowledge about a subject, it will reap incredible benefits.

At some point in your writing career, you will likely be called upon to create characters beyond your own experience, or even beyond anyone's human experience. These could be insane or psychopathic individuals, supernatural beings, animals, alien life forms, or, in my own case, severed heads on spikes. I remember that in my novel _Voice of the Fire_, I included a chapter about one of the Gunpowder Plotters from Northampton, Francis Tresham, who informed on the others and was allowed to die of a disease in the Tower of London rather than being executed like Guy Fawkes. His head was put on a spike at the end of Sheep Street in Northampton. I thought it might be interesting to tell a whole story from the point of view of his decomposing head. You have to ask yourself what that experience would be like. Even though severed heads aren't conscious, I imagined my brain having long since disintegrated, leaving just a few clinging relics inside the skull. What if one of them started itching? Perhaps I would contrive to rock my skull back and forth on the spike to scratch it. I wouldn't be able to see because the birds would have eaten my eyes, or hear because they'd have my ears, but for the story's purposes, I imagined a very limited perspective. If you can imagine yourself into that space and make it seem realistic, you will create a real and unrepeatable experience for your readers.

When dealing with characters in general, it is advisable to always listen to them, allow them their integrity, and let them have their way in the narrative. If a character really wants to do something, let them do it, and no matter how horribly the narrative requires you to treat them, maintain that integrity. Treat them as if they are your friends, albeit friends you regrettably sometimes have to kill. For instance, if you are writing a terrifying serial killer, try to get inside their personality to create a rounded individual. When I was writing _V for Vendetta_ in 1980 and 1981, I had to portray the fascist government controlling Britain in the then-future year of 1997. As a third-generation anti-fascist who absolutely abhors Nazis, I didn't want to reduce my villains to two-dimensional cartoons with monocles and University of Heidelberg dueling scars. I had to remember that Nazis didn't arrive from space; they were ordinary bakers, street cleaners, cobblers, and schoolteachers who had a terrible national experience. If this were happening in England, they would be ordinary people. So, I gave each of them a different reason for their actions: some were good people trying to do their best in a horrible regime, some were frightened, and some actively enjoyed the feeling of power. It is crucial to understand what is inside your villains and empathize with them, because if you aren't careful, they become completely predictable, two-dimensional travesties. If your heroes are to have any weight and resonance, your villains must have the same.

The importance of making your characters three-dimensional cannot be overstated. In comics, a one-dimensional character is simply a hero or a villain, a "goodie" or a "baddie" with no motivations other than their alignment. A two-dimensional character, a formula popularized around the early 1960s by Stan Lee, adds a slight personality problem or cosmetic flaw—like a good guy who can't find a girlfriend or has a bad leg. This doesn't make them resemble human beings; it just gives them a superficial trait. To create a truly three-dimensional character, you must go much further. You need to know them from the inside out, understanding why they do things and knowing all the elements of their life that shaped them, giving them complete agency and their own agendas. This reminds me of working on my film _The Show_ with Mitch Jenkins. We gave narratives, story arcs, and distinct personalities to an incredible number of characters, deciding to have no secondary characters. We directed our fine actors by telling them to play the scene as if the whole film was about them. That is how it works in real life: we all think the whole film is about us, and we are the center of the narrative. The interaction of characters is generally just a bunch of people colliding while all believing the story is entirely about them. You will not achieve that degree of realism and involvement unless you allow your characters the full complexity of an ordinary human being.

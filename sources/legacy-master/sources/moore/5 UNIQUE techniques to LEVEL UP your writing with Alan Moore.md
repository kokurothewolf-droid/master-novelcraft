
If you hope to guide your reader through the entire length of your story from beginning to end without their interest waning or lagging, it is well advised to use inventive language. Try to put your words together in ways they have not necessarily been combined before. Avoid clichés and phrases that every other writer has used. Constantly making your language more adventurous and surprising is a major element of keeping the reader engaged. As readers progress, their attention will be captured by a surprising image or a startling use of words. One way to achieve this is through the principle of juxtaposition. By putting two words together that initially seem dissonant or discordant, you can occasionally create a kind of beautiful disfigurement.

H.P. Lovecraft has a reputation as an old-fashioned writer who hated modernism in any form. He loathed Gertrude Stein and wrote a funny parody of T.S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* called "Waste Paper," showing he understood modernist poetry even if he professed to despise it. However, I believe Lovecraft was probably a closet modernist. He is often criticized for his ridiculous, overblown use of adjectives, especially when describing a creature he eventually calls "indescribable." My friend, the writer Michael Moorcock, called Lovecraft the "master of overdescribing the indescribable." While I see what Mike means, I don't think that is entirely the truth. Looking closely at Lovecraft, you can see he was using language in a very determined way. He would overload descriptive adjectives upon his creations to make it impossible for the reader to form a clear mental picture. For instance, in his first mention of his most famous monster, Cthulhu, he spends a lot of time telling you three things that Cthulhu *doesn't* quite look like. In *The Dunwich Horror*, when the subhuman creature Wilbur Whateley is finally destroyed in an incident involving dogs in a library, Lovecraft describes the dying body by looking at different parts. He describes one part as covered in thick hair, another as bubbling with tentacles, and notes eyes in the hips. By providing so many conflicting textures, he makes it impossible to assemble these parts into a single, coherent image. This is a deliberate technique to alienate and derange the reader, making them feel that something is fundamentally wrong.

This effective and unusual use of language brings to mind the marvelous tricks William Shakespeare used. With modern brain imaging techniques, we can now observe the patterns of brain activity when people read Shakespeare. It turns out that one of the things people enjoy most about his work is his wordplay. In one instance, Shakespeare uses the phrase, "Wouldst thou lip aboard?" Here, he changes a noun into a verb, using "lip" to mean "kiss." He also takes an adjective and changes it into a noun; in context, "aboard" would mean a loose woman or a harlot. When Shakespeare does this, our neurology goes crazy. It becomes highly excited because something unusual is being done with language, and since practically our entire consciousness is made of language, messing about with it excites our neurons. Shakespeare must have done this instinctively, but he consistently employs this clever wordplay just before an important dramatic denouement. He gets our brains primed and excited, leaving us wanting something thrilling to happen right before he delivers his big dramatic resolutions.

This demonstrates the profound power of language over our minds. If we alter the mode of language or translate it into something we don't quite understand, we become neurologically excited. It is similar to the intricate wordplay of James Joyce, or the practices of magicians and believers in magic who advise using "barbarous names"—casting spells in a language you do not personally understand, whether Latin, English, or something else. When we use language we don't fully comprehend, our minds wander and enter a much more receptive state. I experienced this firsthand when writing my novel *Jerusalem*. For some reason, I decided to write a chapter about James Joyce's daughter, Lucia, during her confinement in a Northampton mental institution for the last forty years of her life. I chose to write it in the linguistic style of her father, which meant inventing nearly every word in the chapter. My spellchecker had a mental breakdown, and it took me a couple of months to write it. After finishing, I couldn't even think in proper English anymore. My mind had become so conditioned to fabricating new words that I was unable to hold a basic conversation. I had to take eighteen months off from publishing an underground comic magazine before I could return to the book and write in standard English again. Fortunately, as a would-be writer, this extreme level of linguistic degeneration is likely not going to be a problem for you anytime soon, but be warned: it could be waiting for you down the line.

One of my favorite literary movements, though perhaps of limited application, is the Oulipo movement. Oulipo is a contraction of the French term *Ouvroir de littérature potentielle*, meaning "workshop of potential literature." In effect, it involves setting strict restraints upon what you can write, which forces you into new creative decisions. The most famous piece of Oulipo fiction is a novel by Georges Perec written entirely without the letter "e," meaning he could only choose words that did not contain it. Imagine how difficult it is to complete a single sentence without using the most common letter in the English language. Taking this further, I once saw a very slim book by Christian Bök titled *Eunoia*. "Eunoia" is an interesting word in itself; it is the etymological opposite of paranoia, meaning "beautiful thinking," and it is the shortest English word containing all five main vowels. In this work, Bök decided that each chapter had to contain a journey by water, a meal, a sexual debauchery, and various other elements, but each chapter could only use a single vowel. As a feat of writing, it is breathtaking. Obviously, this is not something you will be called upon to do, but I advise playing with these constraints occasionally if you have the time. It will stretch your talents into directions you would not have discovered on your own, creating new linguistic possibilities that will enrich all of your prose. People have even used Oulipo techniques to compose music, create visual art, and write specific genres, such as police procedural novels—a sub-movement referred to as the "Oulipopo." It is not for everybody, but if you want to give your writing some exotic spice, I suggest taking a couple of idle hours to play around with these ideas and see where they take you.

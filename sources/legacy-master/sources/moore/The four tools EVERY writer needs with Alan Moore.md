I think that writing and magic are pretty much synonymous; most of the effects of one are completely accomplishable by using the other. From time to time, I may use magical terminology to make a point about writing. This does not mean I am trying to introduce you to a strange cult, nor that you will have to sacrifice goats or worship ugly-looking idols. Rather, there is certain advice given to magicians that I believe is equally useful for an aspiring writer. The first of these concepts is that of the "four weapons." These are four symbolic tools that any magician is advised to possess a full complement of before commencing their career. They correspond to the four suits of the tarot deck: discs (or coins/pentacles), swords, cups, and wands. Each represents one of the four classical elements, as well as a specific human quality or ability necessary to be a successful writer.

Starting from the lowest of these four implements are the discs, coins, or pentacles. They represent the element of earth, which translates to your ability to deal with the material, physical world we all exist in. For a writer, this means paying attention to your physical circumstances; you do not want to starve in a garret, as many writers have done, because it is not a particularly thrilling experience. Try to ensure your material circumstances are at least bearable. Furthermore, you must obtain a knowledge of the physical world. Find out how situations and people work, and how the various facets of the material world fit together. Study history, physical science, and the inner workings of institutions like hospitals. Understanding these tangible realities will save you an awful lot of embarrassment and make you a significantly better writer.

The next weapon in ascending order is the sword, which represents the element of air and the human quality of intellect. Specifically, the sword symbolizes the discriminatory intellect. It is the mind equipped with a sharp, cutting edge that enables you to distinguish between a good idea and a bad one, or to differentiate clearly between concepts. This discriminative ability will be incredibly useful throughout your writing career. Moreover, the pursuit of intellect itself is vital; expanding your intellect directly expands your capacity to write about the world with depth and clarity.

Moving on to the next most important of the four magical weapons, we find the cup. The cup represents the element of water, and thus symbolizes human emotion, particularly compassion. When creating characters, you must feel compassionate toward them. Even if you are writing about the most hateful villain possible, you need to understand them. If you are going to put words in their mouth, you must comprehend them as a human being. The key to this is compassion—the ability to think yourself into somebody else’s shoes. For both a magician and a writer, keeping the cup and the compassion it represents continually within reach is absolutely essential.

The most important of the four weapons is the wand. The wand represents the element of fire, which corresponds to the human spirit, or more specifically, the human will. This is the most crucial weapon because it is the one that should direct the other three. It is the center of the human self—the core that possesses intellect, emotion, and physical circumstances, and actively directs them all. For a writer, will is the single most important element. Without it, you might have the best ideas in the world, but you will only get two chapters into a novel before abandoning it for an even better idea. Without the application of will, you can easily end up, after thirty years of writing, with nothing but a stack of uncompleted manuscripts. However, if you focus your will upon your imagination, you can actually bring your ideas into materialization, making them exist in the real world where the rest of us can read them.

It is obviously a lot easier to say "get in touch with your will" than it is to actually do it. When normal avenues of education were forbidden to me at the age of seventeen, I began to realize I was at a disadvantage. If I wanted to have the career I dreamed of as a self-supporting writer and creative individual, I would have to make a deliberate attempt to achieve it. Around the age of twenty-four or twenty-five, I decided to leave work and live off the then non-existent mercies of the benefit system until I could establish myself. However, I soon realized that this approach was merely an attempt to avoid being judged, and it was ultimately dooming any attempt at becoming a writer. If you do not finish your work and give it to someone to appraise, it will simply never get published; you are only kidding yourself. I realized that no one was going to help me become a writer, but equally, no one was trying to prevent me from becoming one. It was entirely down to me.

Funnily enough, as soon as I made that decision to take responsibility, I thought, "Why don't I have a look and see what papers publish comic strips?" I sent a couple of sample episodes to a current music paper called *Sounds*, and luckily, I received a telegram in reply—since we did not have phones in those days—telling me they would like to start running the script. This was my first professional work, and it enabled me to build the rest of my career purely by making the decision to take my life and my responsibilities into my own hands. I would say that this is not just the secret of being a good writer, but of living a purposeful life. These are the four weapons, and I would strongly advise you not to neglect any single one of them.

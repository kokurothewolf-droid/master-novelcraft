
Reading a book is fundamentally a neurological phenomenon. The words on the page trigger specific responses in our brains, and while we read at a normal pace, a multitude of important cognitive processes occur within fractions of a second. The science fiction writer Samuel R. Delaney brilliantly illustrated this by analyzing the first line of one of his novels: "The red sun is high, the blue low." He explained that each word redefines the ones that came before it. The word "the" is merely a gray oval hanging in space. Adding "red" turns the oval red. Adding "sun" transforms it into a circle that has risen slightly. The present-tense verb "is" immediately engages us, making the scene feel immediate rather than distanced. "High" moves the red circle to the top of the imaginary picture. Then, a new gray oval appears with the second "the," which turns "blue" and finally "low." In just seven words, we are instantly transported to an entirely alien landscape with two suns casting shadows in different directions, likely creating violet intersections. All of this is accomplished with seven words in the right order.

Why do words impact us so profoundly? This likely has something to do with a relatively recently discovered phenomenon called mirror neurons. We all have these neurons in our brains, and if we watch someone rowing a boat, the exact same areas in our brains light up as if we were rowing the boat ourselves. We experience a ghostly neurological echo of the actions we observe, whether in real life or in a film. For example, if I watch a film where someone is in danger of falling from a great height, my palms immediately start to perspire and the soles of my feet cringe. It is a genuinely anxious and unpleasant physical experience. Crucially, if someone writes compellingly enough about such a scene, I will have the exact same physical reaction. When we interact with writing, something quite unusual and deeply embodied is going on.

This brings to mind the marvelous tricks William Shakespeare used. With modern brain imaging techniques, we can observe the patterns of brain activity when people read his work, and it turns out that one of the things people enjoy most is his wordplay. Consider the phrase, "Wouldst thou lip aboard?" Here, Shakespeare changes a noun into a verb, using "lip" to mean "kiss." He also takes an adjective and changes it into a noun; in context, "aboard" would mean a loose woman or a harlot. When Shakespeare does this, our neurology goes crazy. It becomes highly excited because something unusual is being done with language, and since practically our entire consciousness is made of language, messing about with it excites our neurons. He must have done this instinctively, but he consistently employs this clever wordplay just before an important dramatic denouement. He gets our brains primed and excited, leaving us wanting something thrilling to happen right before he delivers his big dramatic resolutions.

This demonstrates the profound power of language over our minds. If we alter the mode of language or translate it into something we do not quite understand, we become neurologically excited. Therefore, you should try to put your words together in ways they have not necessarily been combined before. Avoid clichés and phrases that every other writer has used. Constantly making your language more adventurous and surprising is a major element of keeping the reader engaged. One way to achieve this is through the principle of juxtaposition. By putting two words together that initially seem dissonant or discordant, you can occasionally create a kind of "beautiful disfigurement." Writers like my friend Steve Aylett use language wonderfully and inventively to great effect; his prose is hilarious and completely hallucinatory, putting words, phrases, and concepts together in extraordinary ways that guarantee an enthralling read.

This intricate manipulation of words is akin to the writings of James Joyce, or even to the practices of magicians and believers in magic who advise using "barbarous names." This means casting spells in a language you do not personally understand, whether it is Latin, English, or something else. The important thing is that when we use language we do not fully comprehend, our minds wander and enter a much more receptive state. I experienced this firsthand when writing my novel *Jerusalem*. I decided to write a chapter about James Joyce’s daughter, Lucia, during her confinement in a Northampton mental institution, and I chose to write it in the linguistic style of her father. This meant inventing nearly every word in the chapter. My spellchecker had a mental breakdown, and it took me a couple of months to write. After finishing, I couldn't even think in proper English anymore. My mind had become so conditioned to fabricating new words that I was unable to hold a basic conversation. I had to take eighteen months off from publishing an underground magazine before I could return to the book and write in standard English again. Fortunately, as a would-be writer, this extreme level of linguistic degeneration is likely not going to be a problem for you anytime soon, but be warned: it could be waiting for you down the line.

H.P. Lovecraft has a reputation as an old-fashioned writer who hated modernism in any form. He loathed Gertrude Stein and wrote a funny parody of T.S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* called "Waste Paper," showing he understood modernist poetry even if he professed to despise it. However, I believe Lovecraft was probably a closet modernist. He is often criticized for his ridiculous, overblown use of adjectives, especially when describing a creature he eventually calls "indescribable." My friend, the writer Michael Moorcock, called Lovecraft the "master of overdescribing the indescribable." While I see what Mike means, I don't think that is entirely the truth. Looking closely at Lovecraft, you can see he was using language in a very determined way. He would overload descriptive adjectives upon his creations to make it impossible for the reader to form a clear mental picture. For instance, in his first mention of Cthulhu, he spends a lot of time telling you three things that Cthulhu *doesn't* quite look like. In *The Dunwich Horror*, when the subhuman creature Wilbur Whateley is destroyed, Lovecraft describes the dying body by looking at different parts: one covered in thick hair, another bubbling with tentacles, and eyes in the hips. By providing so many conflicting textures, he makes it impossible to assemble these parts into a single, coherent image. This is a deliberate technique to alienate and estrange the reader, making them feel that something is fundamentally wrong.

There are certainly extremes that writers have gone to in order to achieve these effects, such as the Oulipo movement (*Ouvroir de littérature potentielle*, or "workshop of potential literature"), which involves setting strict restraints upon what you can write to force new creative decisions. But beyond formal constraints, we must consider how writing affects our minds. It is worth remembering the concept George Orwell introduced in *1984*: Newspeak. This was a new form of English engineered by the state with very few words, designed so that the actual consciousness of the people using the language was limited, restricting the concepts they could even think. I think that the less language you have, the more easily controlled you are. But if that is true—and I fear it probably is—must not the converse also be true? If you expand your vocabulary and your use of language, does that not mean that your consciousness will expand as well, and the consciousness of your readers along with it? That is something worth thinking about.
